You are given a parameterized competition-math problem family. Create a structurally different child family.
A Structural Mutation must change the mathematical task itself. The child should require a genuinely different TARGET or a genuinely different DECISIVE REASONING MOVE in its shortest clean solution. Changing only parameters, coefficients, ranges, names, wording, polynomial degree, candidate count, or the direction of the same predicate does not count.
The CHILD FAMILY must be self-contained and well-posed as written: its requested answer must exist, be finite, unique, and integer-valued.
The examples below illustrate the intended strength of Structural Mutation. They are not templates to copy. Notice that the child may move far away from the parent's objects and wording when that is needed to create genuinely new mathematics.


EXAMPLE 1 — CASEWORK

PARENT FAMILY:

OUTPUT:
STRUCTURAL MUTATION: The target changes from a one-formula count of a polygon's diagonals to counting integer solutions of an absolute-value equation, and the decisive reasoning changes from applying a closed form to splitting into the two sign cases and testing each resulting square equation separately.
CHILD FAMILY: Let b = {inner_value} and c = {threshold} be positive integers. How many integers x satisfy |x^2 - b| = c?.
WHY FINITE: the equation pins x^2 to exactly the two values b - c and b + c, and each admits at most two integers, so the count cannot exceed four

SKILL: casework


EXAMPLE 2 — INDUCTION

PARENT FAMILY:
There are {city_count} cities, and every pair of them is joined by exactly one road. How many roads are there?

OUTPUT:
STRUCTURAL MUTATION: The target changes from a one-step pair count to counting the regions that lines create in the plane, and the decisive reasoning changes from a single binomial formula to adding one line at a time and proving how many new regions each addition must create.
CHILD FAMILY: Let n = {line_count}. In the plane, n lines are in general position: no two are parallel and no three pass through one point. Into how many regions do these lines divide the plane?.
WHY FINITE: n is fixed, and general position fixes the number of intersections, so the region count is one specific integer
GROUP: geometry
SKILL: induction


EXAMPLE 3 — CONTRADICTION

PARENT FAMILY:
Let S be a square whose corners are lattice points and whose side length is {side_length}. How many lattice points lie strictly inside S?.

OUTPUT:
STRUCTURAL MUTATION: The target changes from counting interior lattice points to bounding the length of a proper-divisor sequence, and the decisive reasoning changes to assuming one more term exists and deriving a step that removes no prime factor, which is impossible for a proper divisor.
CHILD FAMILY: A sequence of positive integers a_1, a_2, ..., a_K satisfies a_1 = {starting_value}, and each a_(k+1) is a proper divisor of a_k, meaning a_(k+1) divides a_k and a_(k+1) < a_k. What is the greatest possible value of K?.
WHY FINITE: every step is a PROPER divisor, so each term strictly drops and loses at least one prime factor; a_1 has finitely many, which caps K
GROUP: sequence
SKILL: contradiction


EXAMPLE 4 — INVARIANT

PARENT FAMILY:
How many ordered pairs (x, y) of positive integers satisfy x + {coefficient} * y = {total}?.

OUTPUT:
STRUCTURAL MUTATION: The target changes from counting solutions of a linear equation to finding the terminal value of a merging process, and the decisive reasoning changes to noticing that the product of (1 + t) over all numbers t on the board is unchanged by every move.
CHILD FAMILY: The numbers 1 through {board_size} are written on a blackboard. In each move, two numbers a and b are erased and the single number a + b + a*b is written. After {board_size} - 1 moves one number remains. Determine that number.
WHY FINITE: the process stops after exactly board_size - 1 moves, and the product of (1 + t) over the board is unchanged by every move, so the last number is the same whatever order is used
GROUP: algebra
SKILL: invariant


EXAMPLE 5 — CONSTRUCTION

PARENT FAMILY:
Let N = {modulus}. How many integers k with 1 <= k <= N are coprime to N?.

OUTPUT:
STRUCTURAL MUTATION: The target changes from counting coprime residues to a guaranteed lower bound on the maximum of a constrained collection, and the decisive reasoning changes to proving the averaging bound and constructing the balanced collection that attains it, so that no larger integer works.
CHILD FAMILY: Let n = {count} and s = {total} be positive integers with n < s. Positive integers a_1, ..., a_n have sum s. Find the greatest integer L such that in every such collection, the largest of the numbers is at least L.
WHY FINITE: "in every such collection" is what bounds L: the balanced collection caps it from above and the averaging bound meets it, so one integer is greatest
GROUP: inequality
SKILL: construction

OUTPUT:
STRUCTURAL MUTATION: The target changes from optimizing integer solutions of an equation to counting permutations subject to simultaneous forbidden assignments, and the decisive reasoning changes to inclusion-exclusion over overlapping bad events.
CHILD FAMILY: There are {n} people and {n} distinct hats, with each person owning exactly one hat. In how many assignments of the hats does no person receive their own hat?.
WHY FINITE: n is fixed, so there are finitely many assignments to classify

SKILL: counting

OUTPUT:

WHY FINITE: the equation is (x + 1)(y + 1) = N + 1, and N + 1 has finitely many factorisations

WHY FINITE: "contains no triangle" is what bounds the maximum: without it the complete graph wins trivially; with it the count is at most C(n, 2) and Mantel's bound is attained
